# Week 06 — MOdeling

This week I built the ger in Blender and prepared Bataa, the guide who introduces the grassland to the player.

## Ger references

I chose a familiar Mongolian ger silhouette: a circular wall, sloping roof, central crown and wooden entrance. Its portable frame suits a household that moves between pastures. Felt provides insulation, the outer cover protects the enclosure, and the crown allows light and ventilation. These features support use in cold and windy conditions, with ventilation important in warmer weather (UNESCO, 2013; UNESCO-ICHCAP, 2013).

*Form and proportions: Fischer (2010), [photograph and CC BY-SA 2.0 licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mongolian_Ger.jpg).*

![White ger with blue decorative borders](https://www.mnb.mn/uploads/202404/news/thumb/0053657bc9191578b09af5b60bbb7896_x3.jpg)

*Exterior reference: [Mongolian National Broadcaster, ger exhibition (2024)](https://www.mnb.mn/i/310642).*

![Ger with a raised roof cap and blue borders](https://www.wendywutours.com.au/resource/upload/376/mongolian-yurt.jpg)

*Raised cap and blue border reference: [Wendy Wu Tours, ger accommodation](https://www.wendywutours.com.au/mongolia/tours/mongolia-naadam-festival.htm). This tourism example is a closer visual comparison for the model's decorative exterior.*

The white cover, blue borders and wooden door connect these references to my model. Its raised roof cap is a stylised feature. The curled decoration resembles the Mongolian *ugalz* scroll tradition, used in household objects and clothing; the exact blue motif in my design has no verified historical attribution (National Center for Cultural Heritage of Mongolia, 2019).

## Building and fixing the ger

I assembled the wall, roof sections, crown and entrance in Blender, checking the model in wireframe, from above and as a solid form. The basic geometry was manageable. Keeping the decorative bands level around a curved wall was harder.

## Character references and design

For Bataa, I looked at the long robe, belted waist, hat and boots associated with the Mongolian *deel* (UNESCO, 2010). The photographs below helped distinguish practical everyday clothing from more decorative versions.

![Herder wearing a grey deel and dark hat](https://www.unesco.mn/files/img/0.624364001730173336.jpg)

*Everyday clothing reference: [Mongolian National Commission for UNESCO (2024)](https://www.unesco.mn/20/nitem).*

![Dariganga clothing exhibited in the National Museum of Mongolia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a2/Dariganga_Traditional_Clothing_%2835579174491%29.jpg)

*Robe, sash and boots: Gary Todd (2017), Dariganga clothing, National Museum of Mongolia. [Photograph, CC0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dariganga_Traditional_Clothing_%2835579174491%29.jpg). Used to compare garment construction, rather than assign Bataa a specific regional costume.*

I settled on a cream robe, blue trim, leather belt and boots. The light clothing stands out against the grass, and the trim connects him visually to the ger. The hat, hairstyle and accessories remain stylised design choices. The costume sheet explores alternatives; the turnaround clarifies the front, side and back for 3D modelling.

## Character production and my limits

I used **Tripo AI for the base character** and **Mixamo for body rigging and animation**. My knowledge of anatomy and facial muscles is still limited, and my own modelling attempts were not at the quality I wanted. I estimated that building the full character to that standard would take at least a month. With the available time, I prioritised the interaction and learning experience.
