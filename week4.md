# Week 04 — Researching a Living Pasture

This week I focused on how games make a world feel like a system. Ian Cheng’s *Emissaries* is useful to think with: its living simulation keeps changing, while a story gives the viewer something to follow. *Rain World* offers another reference: its creatures search for food and shelter even when the player is not directing them. I want to learn from this sense of an active world, while keeping my own game grounded in Mongolian pastoral decisions rather than survival or fantasy [1, 2].

## The problem I want to explore

I am concerned that cultural knowledge can stay abstract when it is presented only as facts. I want learners to notice how several conditions affect one another, and to explain why a place may or may not work for a herding household.

**Research question:** How might an embodied simulation of Mongolian pastoral decision-making help design learners develop cross-cultural contextual sensitivity by reading the relationships between weather, pasture, water, terrain, and livestock?

## Early game idea

The player explores a grassland, observes environmental clues and animal behaviour, then decides whether to stay or move camp. The world responds to changing conditions. The decision is not a quiz with one correct answer; the player should be able to describe what they noticed and what they prioritised.

I am considering two difficulty levels. In the **simple version**, weather and environmental clues are clear, resources are easier to compare, and the game gives more guidance. In the **challenging version**, weather changes over time and clues can compete: a grassy area may be far from water, or a convenient camp may expose animals to harsher conditions. Difficulty would come from interpreting connected conditions, not from arbitrary surprises.

## What needs evidence

Published research on herding in Mongolia and the Altay links livestock movement and camp use to season, forage availability, water access, temperature, snow, wind, and terrain [3, 4]. Research on Mongolian steppe vegetation commonly measures plant cover and above-ground biomass; some field studies also measure plant height [5, 6]. I should therefore avoid treating tall grass as a simple synonym for good pasture. The game’s grass height and density need to be tied to a specific region, season, and documented vegetation measures.

Weather can affect grazing and movement. For example, research in the Dzungarian Gobi reports that high temperatures can reduce small livestock grazing activity, while livestock move less during cold periods [3]. I can express these effects through changed grazing, movement, or shelter-seeking. If animals look restless, that will be a visual cue in the simulation, not a claim that a particular emotion has been measured.

Water should also be a real decision factor, not just scenery. Research on Mongolian steppe hydrology identifies seasonal feed shortages and the condition of the well network as constraints on pastoralism [7]. I need more reading before setting exact distances, quantities, or thresholds.

For this early design stage, environmental values and behaviour rules will come from published literature and research datasets. I have not used structured interviews or collected primary interview data to set them. I will keep a source beside each rule as the model develops.
